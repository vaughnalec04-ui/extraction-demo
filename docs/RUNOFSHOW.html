The code was written with Claude Opus 5 as a pair, the documents were drafted with Claude and Gemini, and every design decision is mine. I'll show you the ones that matter and why I made them.

Two more things up front. Everything ran on the free Gemini API tier, on purpose, and that shaped the design in a way I'll come back to. And on time: the implementation was about [your hours] hours. The rest of the calendar was waiting on quota, generating documents, and writing.

2. The problem 0:50 to 1:40 · Tab 1, BLUF and Context

Do: point at the BLUF box, then scroll slightly to Context.

Meridian gets about forty thousand scanned claims a month. They want six payment-critical fields pulled off each page: claim id, policy number, claimant, provider, date of service, total amount. Three requirements. Ninety-seven percent field accuracy. Under two cents a document. And the system has to flag what it isn't sure about instead of guessing, because a wrong payout costs far more than a human review.

That third one is the interesting one. Reading fields off a scan is mostly solved. Knowing when you got it wrong is not. So the harness is built around that question.

3. Architecture 1:40 to 3:40 · Tab 1, section 1

Do: scroll to 1.1 Flow. Read the arrow line with your finger on it. Then 1.3, the trade-offs table.

Here's the flow. Labels first. I write the ground truth from a fixed seed, then render each page from its label, so no label ever depends on a model. Then the extractor: Gemini structured output against a response schema, one nullable value and one confidence per field. Every raw response goes into a cache that's committed to the repo. And the harness scores the cache. It never calls the API.

That split is the main architectural decision. Extraction and scoring are two programs. Once the responses are cached, every threshold sweep is free, tuning is reproducible against a fixed set of answers, and the demo you're about to see runs offline with identical numbers every time. The cache key is the model, the prompt version, the schema, and the image bytes, and both programs recompute it on read. If you regenerate a page and try to score it against an old response, it refuses. That failure doesn't crash, it gives you plausible wrong numbers, so it gets a check.

Four configurations come out of the same cached calls. One reader alone. The second reader alone. A cascade, where a page goes to the second reader when the first one's confidence is low, which is the standard cost saver. And double-keying: both readers on every page, and any field where they disagree goes to review. That last one comes from how claims processors already work. Two people key the same document, a supervisor settles disagreements. I automated the control they already trust.

Now the free tier. The original design escalated to a Flash-class model. Every Flash model I ran toward evaluation volume stopped after about twenty requests and returned 429s. So the second reader became an independent lite-class model instead. For a disagreement signal, independence matters more than raw capability, so the evaluation survived. The cost is that the cascade has nothing stronger to escalate to, and you'll see that in the results.

6. The rule in code 6:50 to 7:40 · Tab 5

Do: Tab 5. The highlighted lines are the double-key branch.

Here's the whole idea in ten lines. For each field, take the primary's value and the verifier's value, normalize both, so a dollar sign or a comma isn't a disagreement, and compare. Agree, and the primary's value goes out. Disagree, and the field is marked abstained and goes to the queue. The confidence we record is the lower of the two, though nothing depends on it. That's the point. This configuration needs no self-assessment from either model.

7. What the eval says, and doesn't 7:40 to 9:10 · Terminal, then Tab 1 section 2.3

Do: terminal, scroll to the primary_solo block and find the line "calibration ... DEGENERATE: not a curve". Then Tab 1, the Cascade paragraph.

Three findings I want to be straight about. First, self-reported confidence. The second reader said 1.0 on all two hundred forty fields. The primary said 1.0 on two hundred fourteen. The harness flags the calibration curve as degenerate, because there's one bin with enough points to be a bin. You can't build a gate on a constant.

Second, the cascade. On the tuning split, no escalation threshold improved accuracy, and sending every page to the second reader made it worse, 98.75 down to 97.92, at 1.7 times the cost. The second reader isn't stronger. So tuning set the threshold to zero and the cascade is just the primary. That result is about these two readers, and it's the direct consequence of the quota problem. With a Flash-class second reader I'd rerun it.

Third, the abstention signal itself. Eight flags in two hundred forty fields. Two were real errors. Precision 25 percent, and the interval runs from 7 to 59. So I can't tell you yet whether double-keying pays for itself. I can tell you exactly what would settle it: about five hundred of Meridian's real documents, weighted toward the exceptions their contractors already flag, through this harness unchanged. The gates are written down in the briefing.

8. From an eval to a benchmark 9:10 to 10:20 · Tab 2, README Dataset section

Do: Tab 2. Scroll the README to the Dataset table (five strata) and stop there.

The reason I built it this way is that most of it generalizes into a benchmark for extraction with abstention. The pieces: a label-first generator with named strata, so difficulty is designed rather than discovered. A committed response cache, so anyone can reproduce the scoring without spending a cent. A fixed metric set: accuracy never without coverage, three error kinds never pooled, abstention precision from recorded counterfactuals, intervals on everything. Paired comparison between systems on the same documents, so nobody compares overlapping error bars. And reconciliation, does the itemisation add up to the total, because adjudication is the job under the transcription.

The findings tell you what such a benchmark has to contain. Out-of-distribution pages that bait a hallucination, because that's where the two readers differed. Degraded scans, because that's the only stratum with errors. And the reported artifact should be the whole cost-accuracy frontier rather than one number, because Meridian's decision is a trade between coverage and review load. What's missing is real documents. The synthetic failure distribution is my guess at theirs.

9. Friction, for the Gemini teams 10:20 to 11:30 · Tab 6

Do: Tab 6 opens at F-007. Read the confidence counts. Scroll down to F-010.

The friction log has eleven entries, written as things happened, with the error text as it appeared. Four matter most. F-007: self-reported confidence is near-constant and log probabilities aren't available for structured output. For any partner whose requirement is "know when you're unsure", a calibrated confidence output is the missing primitive. Right now a second model fills that gap at extra cost.

F-010: the free tier stopped every Flash model I pushed toward evaluation volume after about twenty requests. The whole evaluation costs about a dollar at list price. That's the tier a partner uses to decide whether Gemini fits, and it can't support an evaluation. A per-project evaluation allowance would fix it.

Two smaller ones. The same 429 message covers a rate limit and an exhausted entitlement, so a client can't tell whether to wait or stop. And batch create returns a failed precondition without naming the precondition, so I never got to measure the half-price batch mode.

If they ask

Short spoken answers. Know these without notes; the committee's hire signal depends on it.

QuestionAnswer
Why labels before documents?If labels come from a model, you're measuring agreement with that model. Writing the label first and rendering the page from it means the truth is independent of anything being tested, and I can regenerate the corpus byte for byte from the seed.
Why Wilson intervals?My proportions sit near 1.0 with n between 40 and 240. The normal approximation gives intervals that go above 100 percent there. Wilson stays inside 0 to 1 and behaves at small n.
What is the paired comparison doing?Every configuration is scored on the same documents, so the difference is paired. McNemar's exact test counts the instances where one system was wrong and the other right. The bootstrap resamples whole documents, because six fields on one page share the same scan and aren't independent.
Why did the cascade collapse to zero?The objective maximises coverage subject to the 97 percent bar, tie-break on cost. Escalation doesn't change coverage and adds cost, and on tune it never raised accuracy because the second reader isn't stronger. So zero was the right threshold for these two readers.
What's in the cache key, and why not the prompt text?Model, prompt version, schema hash, image hash, run index, temperature tag. The prompt text isn't hashed in because changing it would orphan the committed cache, so a test pins the prompt's hash to its version and fails if the text changes without a bump.
What would you watch in production?Disagreement rate against its 3.3 percent baseline, daily. Adjudicator rulings on flagged items, which make abstention precision a live number. A weekly re-score of two hundred documents through the same harness, with a stop on auto-pay if the lower bound drops under 97. And the model version string, because the name is an alias.
Why not just pay for the API?I could have, for a few dollars. I kept the free tier because it's what a partner's evaluation actually runs on first, and hitting the quota wall produced the most useful friction item in the log. I'd re-run the cascade on a paid tier with a Flash second reader; that's next step two.
How much of this did the AI do?Claude wrote most of the code with me directing; Claude and Gemini drafted the documents. The design decisions, the metric choices, the double-keying idea, and the decision to report the negative results are mine, and I can walk any line of the harness.
