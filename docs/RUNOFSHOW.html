Meridian Claims — full walkthrough

Written for the mouth, not the page. One line = one breath. Stop at every line break, even mid-thought. Each beat is its own take — record it, flub it, do it again, move on. You're cutting clips together, not performing one unbroken run.

How to shoot it

  • One take per beat. Thirteen beats. A clean 60-second take is easy where a clean 15-minute take is not, and nobody can hear the cuts.
  • Voiceover over a screen recording? Then just read it. Read the whole thing. Nobody can see your eyes, so all that matters is sounding like you're explaining. Read it out loud twice first — the third pass is the one that sounds like you.
  • Face on camera? Put this right under the lens. Look up at the end of each bold phrase.
  • Say numbers loosely. "About ninety-two percent" is better than "ninety-two point three." The exact figure is on the screen behind you.
  • Beats marked "—BEAT—" are two full seconds. They feel endless while you're recording and read as composure on playback. Don't fill them.
  • Record the terminal beat last, and check the command actually ran before you keep the take.
  • Shoot beat 1 last too. First takes are stiff, and the open is the one that has to sound easy.

Mid-size claims processor, and the problem they've got is volume.
About forty thousand documents a month coming in.

This is one of the eighty documents.
Skewed, faded, there's an intake stamp sitting across the total,

Which raises the obvious objection, so let me get in front of it.

So a model contributed vocabulary and layout.
It never contributed an answer.
And if I'd done it the other way round —

PART TWO

The architectural decisions

Six calls, and the reason behind each. Two of them I'd defend hardest, and one of them caught a bug that would have quietly invalidated everything.

Beat 3
take 3

Decision one — schema, not parsing

ScreenScroll to the system flow diagram, section 01
Okay, architecture. Six decisions, and I'll give you the reasoning on each.
First one. Every call goes out with a response schema attached.
I'm not asking for JSON in a prompt and then parsing whatever comes back.
And the reason isn't tidiness, it's what it lets me express.
Every field comes back as a value plus a confidence,
and the value is allowed to be null.
That null matters more than it looks like it should.
Because "this field isn't on the page" is a real answer,
and it's an answer the model can be right or wrong about.
If I'm parsing free text, a missing field and a malformed response
look identical to me. I can't tell "it says the total is absent"
from "it broke and gave me nothing."
With a schema, those are two different outcomes, and I can score both.
Beat 4
take 4

Decision two — extraction and scoring are separate programs

Second decision, and this is the one that shaped how everything else works.
The thing that calls the API and the thing that scores it
are two completely separate programs.
The extractor's only job is to get raw responses and write them to disk.
It doesn't score anything. It doesn't know what a threshold is.
Then the scorer reads those saved responses and never touches the network.

— beat —

Three things fall out of that, and all three are load-bearing.
One. Sweeping a threshold costs nothing.
I can try every possible confidence cutoff against the whole dataset
in about a second, because the responses are already sitting there.
So the tuning is exhaustive instead of me guessing at three values.
Two. The tuning is reproducible.
If I tuned live against the API, and the model updated underneath me,
my thresholds would be fitted to a model that no longer exists
and I'd have no way of knowing.
Three, and this is the one you'll see in a minute —
the demo runs with no API key and no internet,
and gives byte-identical numbers every time.
Beat 5
take 5

Decision three — the cache key, and the bug it caught

Third one, and this is the decision I'd defend hardest,
because it caught something real.
Every cached response is keyed on a hash of everything
that could possibly change the answer.
The model name, the prompt version, the schema, the temperature,
which repeat run it was — and the bytes of the image itself.
That last one is the important bit.

— beat —

Because partway through, I rebuilt the dataset.
New documents, same filenames.
And my first version of this only checked whether a file existed at the path.
So it would have happily handed me the old response
for the new document, and scored it, and printed a number.
Nothing would have crashed. Nothing would have looked wrong.
I'd have had confident results for inputs I never actually sent.
That's the worst failure mode available in this kind of work,
because there's no symptom.
Now a read recomputes the key from the actual image on disk
and refuses if it doesn't match.
And I tested that against the real stale cache, not a fake one.
Beat 6
take 6

Decisions four and five — the unit, and what abstaining means

Fourth. The unit I measure is not the document.
It's the field. One document, six fields, six scores.
That sounds like bookkeeping but it changes the answer.
Document-level accuracy asks "was this page perfect,"
and a page with five right fields and one wrong one
scores the same as a page that was total garbage.
Meridian pays per field. So I score per field.
Fifth decision, and this one's subtle enough that I nearly got it wrong.
There's a difference between the model saying a field is absent
and the system refusing to answer.
The first one is a claim. The model is telling me
there's no total on this page. That can be true or false.
The second one is us putting our hands up
and sending it to a human.
If you collapse those two into one bucket,
you can't measure whether your flagging is any good,
because you've mixed "I know it's blank" in with "I don't know."
So every scored field also records what the answer would have been
if we hadn't flagged it.
Which is the only way to ask the question that actually matters:
of the things we flagged, how many were really going to be wrong?
Beat 7
take 7

Decision six — double-keying instead of escalation

Sixth, and this is the one that changed after I had data.
I started with the obvious design.
Cheap model first, and if it isn't confident, send it to a better model.
Everyone builds that. It's the standard cost-saver.
It doesn't work here, and I'll show you the number in a bit.
So what's there instead is borrowed from how they already work.
In claims processing, the way you catch keying errors
is double-keying. Two operators key the same document separately,
and wherever they disagree, a supervisor looks at it.
So: two models read every document, independently.
They agree, it goes through.
They disagree, it goes to the exception queue Meridian already staffs.
I didn't invent a control. I automated the one their ops team already trusts,
which makes the rollout conversation about throughput
rather than about whether anyone believes the thing.
And it paid for itself in a way I didn't expect.
Some of the test documents aren't claims at all.
A utility bill. A vehicle registration. A timesheet.
The right answer on those is "there's nothing here."
The second reader, on its own, invented five field values on those pages.
It read a utility bill's "amount due" as a claim total. Confidently.
The first reader didn't fall for it.
So the two of them disagreed on every one of the five,
and every one went to the queue instead of into a payment.
That's the case for the second reader.
Not accuracy on the easy pages.
Catching one model's confident invention on a page it should've rejected.

take 9

The reframe, and the thing I got wrong

ScreenTab 3 — the broken claim. Point at the line items, then the total.
My dataset kept coming out too easy, and eventually
that told me something I'd been slow to hear.
I was measuring the wrong task.
Reading a number off a form is basically solved.
Two errors in two hundred and forty fields.
That's not where the difficulty is anymore.
And Meridian doesn't lose money misreading a number.
They lose money paying a claim that doesn't add up.
Look at this one. Line items down the middle.
They sum to about sixty-three hundred.
The total at the bottom says fifty and a half.
One of the line items just isn't in the total.
A person checking this catches it. That's the actual job.
So I added reconciliation. Do the line items sum to the stated total?
And I made about a third of the claims deliberately broken —
a line left out, a line counted twice, two digits swapped,
a surcharge that isn't itemised anywhere.
On the test set, the primary reader caught all twelve of the bad ones.
On the tuning set it caught twelve out of fourteen.
And I want to be careful with that, because twelve isn't many.
The interval on twelve runs from about seventy-six percent up to a hundred.
So the honest version is: the miss rate is somewhere under one in four,
and I can't pin it tighter than that with this many bad claims.
The second reader did let one through.
One claim that didn't add up, marked as fine.
That's a cheque that goes out.
That's the number I'd put in front of Meridian. Not the ninety-nine.

— beat, then slow down —

And here's the part where I was wrong.
I assumed these models would be bad at arithmetic.
That's the received wisdom — don't let a language model do maths.
So I built it to catch that. Two paths.
The model adds the line items up itself and gives me a verdict.
And separately, Python adds up the same numbers the model read
and produces its own verdict.
Identical inputs, two different adders.
So any gap between them has to be the arithmetic.
There was no gap.
The model's arithmetic was a hundred percent. Both models.
And the two paths failed on exactly the same documents.
Every failure was a misread digit on a bad scan
that then poisoned the sum.
It added the wrong numbers, correctly.
So the received wisdom is wrong for this task.
The arithmetic is free and it's reliable.
The bottleneck is reading four digits off a stamped, faded photocopy.
Which is a completely different problem to go and fix.
It's resolution and preprocessing, not reasoning.
And I only know that because I built the version of the experiment
that was capable of proving me wrong.

I should be clear about something that shaped a lot of this.
I built the whole thing on the free tier.
And I want to frame that properly, because I don't think it's a complaint.
My read is that the free tier is doing what it's designed to do.
It's an introduction. It's there so you can try the model,
see the shape of it, decide if it's worth a conversation.
It is not there to let you characterise the model's full capability.
So I'm not going to stand here and tell you the free tier is broken.

because I wrote it all down as it happened.
Eleven entries in the friction log.
All written at the time, with the actual error text.

PART FIVE

From an evaluation to a benchmark

These are not the same thing. The difference is where the research value is.

Beat 12
take 12

Eval versus benchmark, and what's actually interesting here

Last thing, and it's the part I find most interesting.
What I built is an evaluation. It is not a benchmark.
Those get used interchangeably and they're really not the same,
and the difference is where the research value sits.
An evaluation is fitted to one deployment.
Meridian's six fields. Meridian's ninety-seven percent.
Meridian's two cents and their exception queue.
It answers exactly one question — can we ship this, for them, now.
It's allowed to be small and idiosyncratic.
And when the deployment changes, you throw it away. That's fine.
That's what it's for.
A benchmark is a measuring instrument.
Lots of people, lots of models, over years.
It has to survive being optimised against.
Which means it needs things my eval doesn't have.
Headroom, so it doesn't saturate the week a new model ships.
Exact scoring, so there's no annotator drift and no judge model.
And it has to be hard to game by memorising.

— beat —

So — is there a benchmark hiding in here? I think there's one.
And it's not the extraction. It's the reconciliation.
Here's why that task has the right shape.
The answer is a single bit. Does it reconcile, yes or no.
No rubric, no judge model, no human annotation.
The ground truth comes out of the generator that made the page,
so it's exact by construction.
And it's procedurally generated, which means two things.
I can mint an unlimited number of fresh instances —
so contamination stops being a worry,
because next year's test set doesn't exist yet.
And I can turn the difficulty with a dial rather than rebuilding the set.
That dial is the bit I'd actually want a researcher to look at.
The size of the discrepancy is a continuous variable.
A line item left out of a total is a twelve-hundred-dollar gap. That's easy.
Two transposed digits is a ninety-cent gap on a five-thousand-dollar claim.
That's genuinely hard, and it's the one that actually gets paid out.
So instead of a score, you get a curve.
Accuracy against how big the error was.
And a curve tells you where the model breaks down, not just that it does.
You can do the same with scan quality, or the number of line items.
Three independent dials, and you can turn them one at a time.

— beat —

And then the piece I'd argue is the real research question.
Because of how I set it up, this task attributes the failure.
Getting a reconciliation wrong can happen three ways.
You misread the digits. Perception.
You read them right and add them wrong. Computation.
Or you get both right and apply the wrong rule. Decision.
Most benchmarks give you a number that blends all three together,
and you can't tell which one to go and work on.
This one separates them, because I have the model's line items,
the model's sum, my own sum of the model's line items,
and the true sum from the generator.
Four quantities, and the differences between them localise the error.
And when I ran that, the answer was unambiguous.
Computation was perfect. It's all perception.
That's a claim about where the capability ceiling is on this class of task,
and it points at the vision side rather than the reasoning side.
Which is a far more useful thing to hand a researcher
than "the model scored eighty-eight."
The other thing a researcher would want out of this,
and it's the one I couldn't deliver, is calibration.
Right now I can't measure it, because the confidence signal is a constant.
Nearly every prediction lands in one bucket.
My calibration error looks fantastic and it means nothing —
it's what you get when something always says "certain" and is usually right.
But this task is a good place to study that,
and better than most of the ones people use,
because correctness here is exact and cheap and unlimited.
You don't need anyone to label anything.
You can generate ten thousand instances at a known difficulty
and ask whether the model's stated confidence predicts its own correctness.
And on the evidence I've got, it doesn't. At all.
Which is a training question rather than a prompting one —
I tried three different ways to get an uncertainty signal out of these models
and none of them worked.

Not for the recording — prep for the live round

Don't answer these on camera; there's nobody there. Know them cold, don't script them.

"You used models to generate the documents and then tested a model on them."

Right, and the labels are the reason that's okay. The ground truth is a seeded RNG in about forty lines of Python, written to disk before any document exists. A model contributed vocabulary and layout. It never contributed an answer. If I'd generated documents first and then had a model read them to produce labels, I'd be measuring inter-model agreement and calling it accuracy.

"Why two small models instead of escalating to a bigger one?"

I tried, and the free tier kills every Flash model after about twenty requests a day. I migrated across four of them before changing the design instead. And for a disagreement signal what matters is that the readers are independent, not that one's smarter. It cost me the escalation story, not the mechanism.

"Your calibration error is tiny, that's good."

I'd push back on that. It's tiny because it's measuring a constant — nearly every prediction sits in one bucket, so there's nothing there to resolve. That's why the harness marks it degenerate rather than reporting it.

"What would you actually build as a benchmark, concretely?"

Procedurally generated claim pages with a parametric discrepancy: magnitude, scan quality, and line-item count as three independent dials. Report accuracy as a surface over those, not a scalar. Score the verdict bit exactly, and report perception error and computation error separately by comparing the model's sum against my sum of its own extractions. Unlimited fresh instances, so contamination isn't a factor.

"How long did this take?"

The pipeline and harness fit the two hours. Where I went past it was regenerating the dataset twice, and both times the reason is in the README's corpus history — the benchmark wasn't measuring anything, so it wasn't finished.
