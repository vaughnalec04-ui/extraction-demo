Partner Engineering Briefing

Document extraction at Meridian Claims Group

Six payment-critical fields, 40,000 scanned documents a month, and a hard requirement that the system knows when it is unsure.

Engagement  week 2 Workload  claim forms · medical invoices · adjuster narratives · repair estimates Readers  gemini-3.5-flash-lite · gemini-3.1-flash-lite Evidence  240 cached calls scored offline · 200-call throughput sweep

Bottom line

  1. 01The accuracy and cost bars are met, but the margin is thin. 99.17% field-level accuracy [97.0–99.8] — the lower bound sits exactly on Meridian's 97% line. Cost is $0.0013 per document, one fifteenth of budget, and was never the constraint.
  2. 02Extraction is solved; adjudication is where the risk is. Reading the fields is near-perfect. Whether the line items actually sum to the stated total is the question Meridian pays people for, and on that the primary reader caught all 12 inconsistent claims [75.8–100] — a wide interval, and the verifier let one through. Its arithmetic is perfect on every reader; every failure is a misread digit.
  3. 03The “knows when it is unsure” requirement is not met. Self-reported confidence is a constant. Logprobs are disabled on these models and self-consistency produced no disagreement. Three candidate uncertainty signals, three dead ends.
  4. 04Double-keying earns its cost on the pages that matter. The verifier alone invented five field values on documents that were not claims at all. The two readers disagreed on every one, so double_key caught all five and reached 100% [98.4–100] on what it auto-processed, flagging 3.3%.
  5. 05Throughput is quota-bound, not concurrency-bound. Flat at ~8 docs/min from 1 to 8 workers and negative at 16. Forty thousand documents a month is ~83 hours of continuous running with no headroom. Lifting the quota, not tuning the client, is what buys margin.
01

Architecture and system flow

What we built, how a document moves through it, and the decisions that were live rather than obvious.

The design is modelled on the control Meridian already runs. Thirty contractors key these documents today, and the established quality control in claims processing is double-keying: two operators key the same document independently, and disagreements escalate to a supervisor. The system automates that control rather than replacing it with something the operations team has never seen.

Throughput is quota-bound, not concurrency-bound — and 16 workers is slower than 1

Flat from 1 to 8 workers, then negative: sixteen workers returns 12% less throughput than one. The ceiling is account-level requests-per-minute. Client-side parallelism converts into back-off, and past the knee the extra offered load costs throughput outright.

Do not size a queue on worker count. The pool size that matters on this tier is about four. And 40,000 documents/month is ~83 hours of continuous running — it fits inside a month with no headroom for a backlog or a reprocess. Lifting the quota, not optimizing the client, is what buys margin.

The limiter is a single shared gate rather than per-worker back-off. N workers each backing off independently still present N times the offered load to one quota, so they converge and thrash; one gate means a 429 on any worker slows the whole pool.

02

Systematic evaluation

How quality is defined, measured, and monitored for this workload — and where the measurement itself runs out.

Ground truth, and why it is not circular

Every record is authored as JSON from a fixed seed, and each document image is then rendered from its record. Labels are written to disk before any document exists, so no label is ever downstream of a model. 80 documents, split 40 tune / 40 test, frozen before the first API call. Test was scored once.

StratumnWhat it isolates

out-of-distribution8wrong document type, carrying decoy fields and no marker saying so

The first corpus measured nothing, and we said so

Version one was too easy: the primary reader scored 240/240 field-instances, zero errors. With no errors the calibration curve collapses to one bucket and abstention precision is 0/0. Four of the five headline metrics were vacuous.

The benchmark, not the system, was what needed fixing. The corpus was hardened against what bad intake actually looks like — never against a target score — and the v1 result is preserved in the repository rather than quietly replaced.

The cascade escalated nothing — 0.0% of documents

cascade is identical to primary_solo on every metric: same accuracy, same cost, same latency. It escalates when self-reported confidence is low, and self-reported confidence is effectively a constant — 239 of 240 predictions fall in the 0.9–1.0 bucket. The trigger never fires.

The standard cost-saving cascade cannot work on this model, because the signal it depends on carries no information. This was predicted before the run and is reported as measured rather than tuned away.

The verifier invented five values on pages that were not claims

It read a utility bill's “Amount Due” as a claim total and an account number as a claim ID — exactly the bait those documents were built to offer. The primary reader did not fall for it. The two readers disagreed on every one of the five, so double_key caught them all.

This is the clearest single demonstration in the data of why the second reader earns its cost: not accuracy on the easy pages, but catching one reader's confident invention on a page it should have rejected outright.

Reconciliation — does the total match the itemisation?

Reading fields is a transcription task these models have largely solved. Whether the line items actually sum to the stated total is the adjudication task underneath it, and it is what Meridian loses money on. 34 test documents carry an itemisation; 12 are deliberately inconsistent — an omitted line, a double-counted line, a transposed digit, an unitemised surcharge.

Read the interval, not the point. With 12 bad claims the recall interval runs from 76% to 100%. The primary reader caught all 12 on test and 12 of 14 on tune; the honest statement is that the miss rate is somewhere under one in four and cannot be pinned tighter with this many bad claims. The verifier's single false pass is a claim that would have been paid.

The arithmetic finding went against the hypothesis it was built to test

The model is asked to sum its own line items and return a verdict. Python separately sums the same line items the model extracted and returns its own verdict. Identical inputs, two adders — any gap between them is arithmetic. There was no gap. Model arithmetic is 100% on every reader.

Both verdict paths fail on exactly the same documents, and every one is a misread digit on a degraded scan — line items are read correctly 91% of the time. “Never let a model do arithmetic” is the wrong lesson for this task. The arithmetic is free and correct; the money is in reading four digits off a stamped, faded page, which is an image-quality problem with an image-quality fix.

Monitoring in production

  • Disagreement rate is the daily health metric. It needs no labels, so it runs on live traffic from day one. A rise means the input distribution moved — new provider template, new scanner, a fax line degrading.
  • Exception-queue outcomes close the loop. Every flagged document gets a human answer, which is a free label. Feed those back and abstention precision becomes measurable on real traffic rather than on our synthetic corpus.
  • Re-run the frozen harness on every model version change. The cache makes the comparison exact rather than approximate.
  • Temperature 0 is not determinism. Across 3 runs, accuracy moved 0.42 points and ECE more than tripled. Treat any single-run figure as ±0.4.

03

Friction log and engineering signal

Recorded live, with exact error text. Nothing reconstructed from memory — entries we could not verify were dropped rather than written up.

Ten entries were logged. The four below are the ones we would act on, ordered by what they cost this engagement. The full log with reproduction detail is in FRICTION.md.

F-007

Self-reported confidence is degenerate, and it is the whole requirement

core modelinghigh
conf=1.00 : 239  (99.6%)
conf=0.95 :   1  ( 0.4%)

The schema explicitly instructed the model to use the full 0.0–1.0 range and not to default high. It defaulted high anyway. Self-consistency was tried as a substitute — 5 samples at temperature 1.0 on the three hardest documents returned 5/5 identical values, so there was no disagreement to convert into a confidence either.

What we'd want instead — a documented, calibrated confidence primitive. Asking a model to introspect a probability yields a number that looks like a probability and behaves like a constant. For any customer whose requirement is “know when you are unsure”, this is the single most important missing piece.

F-010

Free-tier quota cannot fund an evaluation — the exact workload that precedes adoption

producthigh

Every Flash-tier model exhausts after roughly 20 successful requests, then returns 429 in ~0.2s without attempting inference. Measured across four models in sequence; lite-tier models were unaffected, completing 320 calls with zero errors.

The quota is not the cost. Total spend for this entire evaluation at list price is about one dollar. The free tier is generous enough to try a model and too thin to evaluate one — and evaluation is exactly what a partner does before committing. It cost us the capability-escalation architecture and four model migrations.

What we'd want instead — a quota shaped for evaluation: a one-off allowance of a few thousand requests per project, or free-tier limits expressed per-project-per-day rather than per-model, so a fixed budget can be spent where the developer chooses.

F-003 / F-008

One 429 message covers two unrelated conditions

API surfacemedium
429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED — "You exceeded your current quota,
please check your plan and billing details."

Returned byte-for-byte both when the tier has no entitlement to the model at all and when requests are simply arriving too quickly. One is permanent and needs a billing change; the other clears in seconds. Nothing in the status, the message, or the headers separates them. We lost time re-verifying that a working model had not become entitlement-blocked mid-run.

What we'd want instead — distinct reason values for quota-exhausted versus not-entitled, and a Retry-After header on the transient one. The cheapest fix on this list.

F-002

models.list() advertises models that generateContent refuses

API surfacehigh
404 NOT_FOUND — "This model models/gemini-2.5-flash-lite is no
longer available to new users."

Both models appeared in models.list() on the same key, with generateContent in supported_actions. The discovery endpoint and the inference endpoint disagree about what the key can call. This invalidated a model-tier decision already agreed with the customer.

What we'd want instead — models.list() should reflect per-key availability, or return an available_to_caller flag. A model I cannot call should not be in my model list.

F-011

Batch submission fails an unnamed precondition, while listing batches succeeds

API surfacedocumentationhigh
400 FAILED_PRECONDITION — "Precondition check failed."

Returned identically for a one-part text request and a 40-document multimodal batch, on two models, with and without response_schema — so the payload is not the cause. batches.list() succeeds on the same client, so the endpoint is reachable and authenticated. From inside the program the capability looks present.

The Batch API page states no tier restriction, eligibility requirement, or precondition of any kind, and does not document FAILED_PRECONDITION. Batch probably requires a billed project — but that is our inference, not something the API or the documentation says.

What we'd want instead — name the precondition. This is the least actionable error text in the log: it does not say which precondition, which resource, or what to change. “Batch API requires a project with billing enabled” would have cost zero minutes instead of twenty-five, and belongs in the page's first paragraph.

Commercial signal, unprompted by the build

Flash-tier list pricing is promotional through 2026-12-31 and doubles on 1 January 2027 — $0.75→$1.50 input, $3.75→$7.50 output. Every cost figure in this briefing is computed at the current rate. Meridian's unit economics change in four months regardless of which configuration they choose, and they should plan against the post-January number.

What we would do next

In order. Everything here is instrumentation that already exists; it needs harder inputs, not more code.

  • Ingest 500 real Meridian documents, weighted toward the exceptions their contractors already flag, and re-run this harness unchanged. That is the single highest-value move and it needs no new engineering.
  • Replace confidence-gated escalation with a triage router. The cascade failed because it routed on a broken signal. A cheap first-pass classifier — is this page legible, in-distribution, internally consistent? — routes each document to single-read, double-read, or straight to a human. Since the useful metric is cost per correctly extracted field with the human queue priced in, not cost per document, routing is where the remaining economics live.
  • Close the loop on the exception queue. Every flagged document already gets a human answer. Those are free labels; feeding them back makes abstention precision measurable on live traffic instead of on our synthetic corpus.
  • Re-test the capability tier on a billed key, to settle whether a stronger second reader changes the disagreement signal. Currently unknown.
